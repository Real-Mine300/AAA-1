What is Anemia?

Anemia is a clinical condition resulting from a shortage of either the number of functional erythrocytes, or red blood cells, or the hemoglobin concentration. Hemoglobin is a protein rich in iron that plays a key role in transporting oxygen from the lungs to the tissues. If this oxygen transport system is defective, body tissues become deprived of oxygen, which can increase tiredness, pale skin, and dysfunction of organs.

What causes Anemia?

In many cases, anemia may be caused by genetic factors or may stem from a chronic disease. Iron deficiency anemia is the most common form. The main causes of iron deficiency anemia happen when the body's iron balance is disrupted.

  • Negative iron balance: the body's iron need is greater than the nutritional iron supply. This is common in children and pregnant women because pregnancy increases blood volume and tissue growth, which requires large amounts of iron.
  • Chronic hemorrhage: adults whose blood loss is not obvious are often the ones who most likely get iron deficiency anemia. Common sources include the gastrointestinal tract, ulcers, cancer, or parasite infections such as hookworm in developing countries.
  • Malabsorption syndromes: diseases such as celiac disease or procedures like gastric bypass can interfere with the duodenum's ability to absorb dietary iron.
  • Dietary insufficiency: overdependence on diets low in bioavailable heme iron from animal sources and high in phytates found in some grains can reduce iron absorption.

What are the effects of anemia on society?

Iron deficiency anemia is not only a personal health issue, but also a barrier to global social and economic progress.

  • Cognitive and developmental impairment: untreated iron deficiency anemia during infancy is associated with brain function deficits that can affect thinking, learning, motor skills, and emotional regulation later in life.
  • Diminished economic productivity: fatigue and physical lethargy can reduce work capacity in the adult labor force and may lead to major economic losses.
  • Maternal and neonatal mortality: severe anemia increases the risk of hemorrhage and cardiac failure during childbirth, and it can contribute to low birth weight and infant mortality.

What are the efforts to prevent this?

International health organizations and governments have launched many initiatives to control iron deficiency anemia.

  • Mandatory fortification of staple foods such as wheat, flour, and rice with iron and folic acid.
  • Distribution of ferrous sulfate or newer formulations such as iron sucrose for high risk groups, especially through prenatal clinics.
  • Biofortified crops developed through selective breeding and genetic engineering to raise iron content in staple crops such as pearl millet and beans.
  • Public health education programs that encourage dietary diversity and pairing iron rich meals with vitamin C to improve absorption.
Since parasitic infections can cause significant iron loss, improving sanitation and access to clean water is a fundamental but often overlooked strategy in the fight against anemia.

How can we help in suppressing IDA?

The best approach is to break iron deficiency anemia into different groups and address each with policy, science, and community action.

  • Create targeted social media campaigns that de stigmatize symptoms and promote evidence based nutritional science.
  • Advocate for local production of iron rich crops through sustainable agriculture methods.
  • Educate local organizations on myths about iron deficiency anemia and support myth busting programs.
  • Promote sanitation and clean water access as a direct health intervention.
